# S0.2 — relay crypto: threat model, candidate designs, what each implies for device loss

Draft for a human decision. Options with tradeoffs; nothing chosen. Companion to
`S0.1-OPEN-CORE.md`, which depends on this doc's answer and not the other way round: closing the
relay costs nothing on the payload claim only if the design here holds.

Most of the ground is already covered by three documents on `main`, written 2026-09-07 or earlier,
and this draft does not restate them:

- `docs/encrypted-relay.md` — the agreed transport shape: two outbound WSS legs, an opaque 32-byte
  route id, a separate end-to-end channel, two-factor admission (device channel key plus bearer
  inside the channel), direct-only pairing, the metadata boundary, and the open-core line.
- `docs/relay-noise-options.md` — three candidate suites, wire cost, phone custody obligations,
  and a **decision already waiting on fetzy** since it was written: suite C
  (`Noise_IK_P256_AESGCM_SHA256`) recommended, with A as the alternative.
- `docs/relay-crypto-spike.md` — what the experiment proved and the four things it explicitly did
  not.

What those three do not settle, and what `S0.2` asks for beyond them, is the subject here: the
threat model as one list, forward secrecy as a property of the whole design rather than a footnote,
rotation of the daemon's pinned static key, recovery when a device or the daemon machine is lost,
and the tier boundary `S0.1`'s Option B asks the relay to enforce.

---

## 1. Threat model

Who is assumed hostile, what they can do, and what the design must deny them.

| Adversary | Can | Must not be able to |
|---|---|---|
| **The relay operator** (Domovoi, or whoever runs a compatible server) | see addresses, timing, sizes, route ids, connection counts; delay, drop, reorder, replay ciphertext; deny service | read or forge any accepted plaintext; learn a bearer, a private key, or an error string; make an endpoint accept a peer it did not pair with |
| **A network attacker** between any party and the relay | everything TLS already prevents, plus the relay-operator list if TLS fails | anything more than the relay operator |
| **A holder of a copied bearer** (stolen from a client's storage, a log, a backup) | nothing over the relay without the device's channel key; full client authority over a direct route it can reach | admit itself over the public route |
| **A holder of a device's private channel key** (extracted from a phone with a weak custody boundary, or a laptop disk) | complete an IK handshake as that device | be admitted without that device's current bearer; survive revocation of that device record |
| **A holder of the daemon's private static key** (the machine's disk, a backup, a compromised daemon host) | impersonate the daemon to every device that pins it; decrypt the **first IK message** of past and future sessions from those devices | decrypt transport records of past sessions (ephemeral DH protects them); this is IK's documented limit, see §3 |
| **A holder of the relay registration secret** | claim or hijack the route: deny or misroute | authenticate as either endpoint or decrypt anything |
| **A hostile or compromised pairing peer** (LAN attacker during the direct pairing window) | attempt the 21.5-bit spoken code, five tries online | enrol over the internet: pairing is direct-only and the daemon enforces it |

Out of scope, stated so nobody assumes otherwise: a compromised daemon host has the credentials
and the repositories regardless of the relay; the relay design protects the path, not the endpoint.
Preview, download and print bytes have no encrypted relay path at all and stay direct-only.

## 2. What the relay sees in the clear

Settled in `docs/encrypted-relay.md` §Metadata boundary: addresses, timing, sizes, route ids,
connection counts. Billing by machine-hour needs exactly this and nothing more, which is `S0.3`'s
statement to make.

**Considered and rejected, 2026-09-11: a cleartext frame class.** `S0.1`'s Option B was first
read as "the relay declines to carry terminal frames for a free account", which would need the
relay to tell a terminal frame from a JSON-RPC frame: a cleartext class in the multiplexing
header, bound into the AEAD, and new metadata for `S0.3`. It fails on its own terms. A from-source
client can label terminal bytes as `rpc`, so the check is against the hosted product's own
clients only, the same argument `S0.1` makes against daemon-side checks moved up one layer.

**What replaces it.** `S2.5` already meters machine-hours at the relay, and billing reconciles
against that. So the relay is already counting the thing that costs money. "Free is JSON-RPC
only" was a proxy for "terminal is expensive": long-lived streams, high bandwidth. Machine-hours
plus bytes measures that cost directly instead of inferring it from a label. The tier boundary
is therefore **quantity of relay consumption, not class of frame**: free gets N machine-hours or
N registered machines; paid gets more; terminal works on every tier because the relay neither can
nor needs to tell. That is enforceable with no new metadata, keeps every frame opaque, removes
the `S0.3` addition, and is the product's own rule stated plainly: you pay for infrastructure
consumed. It also closes `S0.4` rather than relocating it: there is no tier claim for anything to
verify, because the boundary is a meter, not a permission.

For this doc the consequence is one line: **the relay sees nothing beyond the settled list**,
under every `S0.1` option.

## 3. Forward secrecy, stated for the whole design

Noise IK gives the transport phase full forward secrecy: every logical connection runs a fresh
handshake with fresh ephemerals on both sides, so compromising either static key later does not
decrypt past transport records. That covers JSON-RPC and terminal bytes.

What IK does **not** give:

- **The first handshake message** (client → daemon) is encrypted only to the daemon's static key.
  A later compromise of that static key decrypts every past first message. The current design
  keeps that payload empty and sends the bearer only in the first *transport* record, after the
  responder's ephemeral is in the transcript. Keep that rule; it is the whole difference between
  "the bearer is forward-secret" and "it is not".
- **The first message is replayable.** A replayed first message produces a handshake the daemon
  will answer; it must not produce any application effect before admission completes. The
  existing rule — daemon output gated on admission — holds this.
- **Identity hiding is one-sided.** The client's static key is encrypted in the first message, so a
  passive observer learns nothing; but an attacker holding the daemon's static key learns which
  device connected. The daemon's identity is never hidden, which is fine: the relay already knows
  which route is which.

**Alternative that closes the first gap: XX instead of IK.** XX sends no static key until after
ephemerals are exchanged, so nothing in the handshake is decryptable by a later static-key
compromise, and it needs no pre-shared responder pin. Cost: one extra round trip (three messages
against two), and the responder pin must still be *verified* after the handshake to stop a
hostile relay substituting its own daemon — so the pin is not saved, only moved. On a phone
reconnecting over cellular, one extra round trip is measurable. Noted as an option; the three
existing docs assume IK and their arithmetic would need redoing.

## 4. Rotation

Three keys rotate on different clocks. Only the third is unaddressed today.

| Key | Rotates when | Mechanism | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bearer | on schedule, on suspicion, on revocation | daemon rotates the record; relay stream closes immediately (`docs/encrypted-relay.md` §Lifecycle) | designed |
| Device channel key | on device loss, on custody-policy change, on suite change | re-pair directly; no export path | designed |
| **Daemon static channel key** | on host compromise, on suite change, on suspicion | **not designed** | open |

The daemon's static public key is pinned by every paired device. Rotating it invalidates every
pin at once. Options:

- **R1 · Re-pair everything.** Simplest. A machine with six paired devices needs six direct
  re-pairs, which means six visits to the LAN, tailnet, or SSH tunnel. Honest, expensive, and the
  only option that also handles a *lost* daemon host (§5).
- **R2 · Signed successor.** The daemon keeps a long-lived Ed25519 *identity* key separate from the
  channel key, and pairing pins the identity key. A new channel key is announced as a statement
  signed by the identity key, delivered over any admitted channel or the relay itself. Devices
  accept the successor without re-pairing. Cost: one more key to custody on the daemon, one more
  signature primitive in the profile, and the identity key is now the thing whose loss forces R1.
  This is what `docs/relay-noise-options.md` §Relationship to skill signature authority is
  circling: the skill-signing authority is already an Ed25519 key on the daemon.
- **R3 · Overlap window.** The daemon accepts handshakes to both the old and new static key for a
  bounded period and announces the new pin inside every admitted channel. Devices that connect
  during the window update; devices that do not must re-pair. Cheaper than R2, no new key, but
  a device that was offline for the whole window is stranded, and "bounded period" is a policy
  number someone has to pick and publish.

R2 is the one that makes a host-compromise response survivable at fleet scale; R3 is the one that
ships soonest. They are not exclusive: R3 is the transport, R2 is what makes the announcement
trustworthy when it arrives via the relay.

## 5. Recovery when something is lost

What the design implies, per loss, under the current docs plus each rotation option.

| Lost | Consequence today | With R2 |
|---|---|---|
| **A phone or laptop** (device) | Owner revokes its device record from any other admitted client or from the daemon host. Its bearer and channel key die with the record; the relay stream closes at once. Nothing to recover: keys are device-only and never exported. The replacement device pairs directly. | same |
| **The daemon's machine** (host disk, or a machine wiped and reinstalled) | Every device's pin points at a key that no longer exists. Every device must re-pair directly (R1). If the disk was *stolen* rather than lost, the thief holds the static key and can impersonate the daemon to those devices until they re-pair — and there is no channel to tell them, because the daemon is gone. | Identity key backed up separately (or held in a hardware token) lets the owner sign a revocation-and-successor statement from a rebuilt host; devices accept the new channel key on next connect. If the identity key was on the same lost disk, back to R1. |
| **The relay** (outage, or Domovoi ceases to operate it) | Loopback, tailnet, and SSH routes are unaffected. Relay-only devices lose reachability, not data or credentials. Under `S0.1` A/B, a third-party relay speaking the open protocol can be substituted; the route descriptor's `endpoint` changes, nothing else. | same |
| **A pairing code** (overheard) | Five online attempts against 21.5 bits inside the direct boundary; useless over the internet because pairing is direct-only. Stored digests must not leave the daemon. | same |

The row that matters for a launch is the second: **a stolen daemon disk is undetectable by the
devices that trust it until they are told**. R2 gives the owner a way to tell them; R1 does not.
Decided 2026-09-11: this row is the launch blocker, and R2 is the answer. The open question on
R2's key is in §7.

## 6. Candidate designs, assembled

The suite choice (A/B/C) is orthogonal to the pattern and rotation choices, so these are the
combinations worth putting in front of fetzy rather than the full product.

| | Design 1 · **IK + C + R1** | Design 2 · **IK + C + R3, R2 later** | Design 3 · **XX + A + R2** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handshake | IK, 2 messages, 243 bytes | IK, 2 messages, 243 bytes | XX, 3 messages, ≈ 180 bytes |
| Suite | P-256 / AES-GCM / SHA-256, Secure Enclave and Android hardware handles | same | X25519 / ChaCha / SHA-256, standard Noise rev 34 |
| Phone custody | protected ECDH handle, proven on Android, iOS unproven | same | requires either a compliant X25519 handle path (not demonstrated) or an explicit custody downgrade |
| Forward secrecy | transport yes; first message no | same | complete |
| Daemon key rotation | re-pair all | overlap window now, signed successor later | signed successor from day one |
| Stolen daemon disk | undetectable until re-pair | R3 cannot help if the host is gone; R2 fixes it when it lands | owner signs revocation from a rebuilt host |
| Review surface | one Noise pattern, one non-standard DH extension (Snow's P-256 profile) | same plus a successor-statement format | standard suite; plus Ed25519 identity and statement format |
| Extra round trip on reconnect | none | none | one |
| Ships | soonest | soon, with a committed follow-up | latest |
| Depends on decisions already pending | fetzy's suite choice in `relay-noise-options.md`, iOS proof policy | same | reversing the phone custody requirement, or proving X25519 handles |

**What each implies for device loss** is the same for all three on the device side (revoke, pair
a replacement). They differ only on daemon-host loss: Design 1 leaves the fleet exposed until every
device re-pairs; Designs 2 and 3 give the owner a signed way to move the fleet.

  not a crypto question, listed because §2 moved it here from a frame class.
- ~~Whether the skill-signing Ed25519 key can be the identity key for R2.~~ **Decided
  2026-09-11: separate keys.** Scope would permit reuse; availability forbids it. R2's value is
  an identity key that can be cold, in a token or an offline backup, because that is what makes
  a stolen disk survivable. A skill-signing key must be warm on every skill push. One key forces
  the identity key warm and destroys the protection R2 exists for.
- **The iOS Secure Enclave proof.** Owned by Claude Code per `relay-noise-options.md` §Production
  integration; gates C on iOS, not on Android.
- **Whether the identity key exists at all**, and if so whether it is the skill-signing key or a
  new one. R2 and Design 3 need it; Design 1 does not.

## 9. Ruling (fetzy, 2026-09-12)

**Suite A, review our own composition (R-A).** With it, `S0.1` is **B** and `S0.6` follows.

The deciding argument is not the $25k to $50k. Suite C buys hardware custody on the machine class
that needs it least and cannot buy it on the one that needs it most. Secure Enclave and Android
Keystore are phones. Phones are clients: they hold no code, no repositories, no credentials beyond
their own bearer. The severe stolen-disk case is `hetzner-cx42`, a rented VPS, someone else's disk,
someone else's hypervisor, and no usable key store regardless of suite. C doubles the review cost
and the platform surface to protect the cheap asset while leaving the expensive one exactly where A
leaves it. R2 is already the answer for the expensive asset, and it does not need P-256: a cold
identity key in an offline backup survives disk theft on a VPS, which no hardware path on that box
can.

**R-A over S-A**, on a narrower margin. Same review money, but S-A adds two to four weeks of our
engineering across three runtimes (napi-rs for the daemon, JSI or uniffi twice for React Native)
and a build surface on iOS and Android that we then own forever. The strongest point for S-A is
that a review is per-commit, so our composition carries a re-review liability S-A's thin shim does
not. But a Noise IK composition is a wire protocol: after launch, changing it is a compatibility
break, so it is frozen and the treadmill is small.

**On the record:** Snow's README still says it has received no formal audit, despite the 2024
Trail of Bits report. If its own maintainers do not treat that report as an audit, S-A must not be
priced as though the core review is free. That weakens S-A further. §7a's table therefore
overstates S-A.

**Conditions.**

1. The phone custody downgrade is stated in its own words below. **Accepted 2026-09-12; the
   ruling is final.**
2. Revisit S-A only if the review finds composition defects, not nonce or state nits. Composition
   defects are the signal that owning these 505 lines was the wrong choice.

### What a stolen phone can do under suite A

Under A there is no hardware key handle. The device's static Noise private key is stored by the
app (Expo SecureStore today, an OS-encrypted string). It is exportable by anything that can run as
the app or read its storage.

**What the key is for.** That phone approves gates. A paired phone is the thing that says yes to
a command an agent is waiting on. A stolen key is a stolen yes.

**What the holder can do.** A stolen phone that is unlocked, or whose OS protection is bypassed,
yields the key. The holder can then connect as that device to every daemon it is paired with and:

- allow or refuse any gate an agent is waiting on, on any of those machines, with the same
  authority the phone had;
- read what a paired device can read live: the thread, the session list, the audit log
  (`audit.query` is an ordinary client call), and any other query the device's permissions
  allow.

The key survives outside the phone. Locking or wiping the phone afterwards does not end the
exposure.

**What the holder cannot do.** Captured relay traffic from before the theft cannot be decrypted:
each connection has fresh ephemerals, so transport forward secrecy holds. The holder cannot
impersonate a daemon to anyone (the daemon static key is not on the phone), cannot reach any
machine the device was not paired to, and cannot exceed the device's permissions on the machines
it can reach.

**What ends it.** Revocation of the device on each daemon, or key rotation under R1/R2. Nothing
else does. Revocation is per daemon and needs the owner to reach that daemon. A machine the owner
cannot reach cannot be revoked, and the holder may be able to reach it when the owner cannot. This
is the same shape as policy on an offline machine: the daemon enforces what it last heard, and
until the owner reaches it, it does not know the phone is gone.

**What remains true after the fact.** Every gate decision is written to that daemon's audit log
with the device id of the credential that made it (`credential.device.id` is the audit actor's
`clientId`; `apps/daemon/src/server.ts:3524`). A stolen phone's approvals are attributable to that
device after the fact, from the daemon or from any other paired client. This does not undo an
approval; it makes it findable.

**What suite C would have added, and A gives up.** Under C the key never leaves the enclave. A
thief with an unlocked phone could use the key while the phone stayed unlocked and in hand, but
could not copy it out; a lock, wipe, or biometric failure would end the exposure without the owner
acting. Under A the owner must act, on every daemon, and cannot act on one they cannot reach.

**The product statement that follows.** A lost phone must be revoked promptly, from another
paired device or the daemon host, on every machine it was paired with. The device list, the
revoke action, and the audit log's device attribution are part of the security model, not
conveniences.

### S0.1 and S0.6

`S0.1` is **B**. The payload claim rests on client-side encryption with a reviewed composition, so
closing the relay server costs the claim nothing. `S0.6`: the relay server goes to a separate
private repository; `packages/protocol/relay/` keeps the wire format and the client side in the
open.

Added 2026-09-13: the relay server is hosted only, permanently. The tier meter lives in the
server, so an open server has no enforcement point; that is the S0.4 argument one layer up, and it
is the reason the decision cannot be traded away later. Hosting is what funds the project.
Self-hosting, if ever, is a paid licence with the meter intact, never a source release. The trust
claim does not depend on the server: payload unreadability is a property of the open clients, and
the metadata claim rests on S6.1's audit.
